The Geometry of SVD and its Application to PCA

Created on November 03, 2026

2026   ·   linear-algebra   machine-learning   ·   math

Singular Value Decomposition (SVD) factors any matrix into a sequence of three geometrically intuitive transformations: a rotation, a scaling, and another rotation. Following Gilbert Strang’s approach in Linear Algebra and Learning from Data, this decomposition forms the foundational bridge between pure linear algebra and data-driven methods like Principal Component Analysis (PCA).

The Geometrical Action of SVD

For any real $m \times n$ matrix $A$, the SVD is given by:

  • $A = U \Sigma V^T$

The matrices are defined by their structural and geometric properties:

  1. $V^T$ is an $n \times n$ orthogonal matrix. Geometrically, it acts as a rotation (or reflection) in the input space $\mathbb{R}^n$.
  2. $\Sigma$ is an $m \times n$ diagonal matrix containing the singular values $\sigma_i$. Geometrically, it scales the rotated axes, stretching or shrinking the space along the coordinate axes.
  3. $U$ is an $m \times m$ orthogonal matrix. Geometrically, it applies a final rotation in the output space $\mathbb{R}^m$.

When we apply $A$ to a vector $x$, the transformation $Ax$ decomposes strictly into these three fundamental steps. The right singular vectors (columns of $V$) form an orthonormal basis for the row space, and the left singular vectors (columns of $U$) form an orthonormal basis for the column space.

Extracting Principal Components

PCA is fundamentally an application of SVD to a mean-centered data matrix. Let $X$ be an $N \times d$ matrix where rows are samples and columns are features, with the empirical mean of each column shifted to zero.

The sample covariance matrix $C$ is computed as:

  • $C = \frac{1}{N-1} X^T X$

If we compute the SVD of our data matrix $X$:

  • $X = U \Sigma V^T$

We can substitute this into the covariance equation to reveal the structural relationship:

  • $C = \frac{1}{N-1} (U \Sigma V^T)^T (U \Sigma V^T)$
  • $C = \frac{1}{N-1} V \Sigma^T U^T U \Sigma V^T$

Because $U$ is an orthogonal matrix, its columns are orthonormal, meaning:

  • $U^T U = I$

Substituting the identity matrix simplifies the covariance matrix to its eigendecomposition:

  • $C = V \left( \frac{\Sigma^2}{N-1} \right) V^T$

This derivation proves that:

  1. The right singular vectors $V$ of the data matrix $X$ are exactly the eigenvectors (principal directions) of the covariance matrix $C$.
  2. The eigenvalues $\lambda_i$ of $C$ are directly proportional to the squared singular values of $X$:
  • $\lambda_i = \frac{\sigma_i^2}{N-1}$

References:

  1. Strang, G. (2019). Linear Algebra and Learning from Data. Wellesley-Cambridge Press.
